I How to read this

Nothing here is urgent. What matters is the order of the steps, and knowing where the one fixed date sits.

Each step has its own short list of things to do. The tasks sit next to the reasons for them, so you do not have to jump around. Ticks are saved in this browser only, so keep the real list somewhere safe.

Small numbers in the text link to the source for that fact↓. Almost all of them are official. Where I worked something out myself instead of copying it, it is marked calc.

I left out prices, interest rates and market averages on purpose. They change every few months, and they would make this page wrong. What stays are the rules that decide things: how much your salary can borrow, which costs you cannot borrow, and what you lose when you turn 35.

II Where you are

The starting point, and what it is enough for.

Good news

Your salary is not the problem

At about €80,000 you can borrow enough for a first house. So what you earn is not what holds you back.

What holds you back is much smaller and easier to fix: a few thousand euros in cash that no bank will lend you, and a permanent contract. You have time for both.

One warning about the 4 to 4.5 rule: it is only a rough guide. The real number comes from tables that change every year, and it moves with interest rates and with any debts you have. So the number to trust is the one an adviser writes down for you.6

Careful

May 2028 leaves less room than it looks

Your 35th birthday comes only a few months after May 2028. This is not a relaxed target with years to spare. It sits close to the limit.

That makes delays expensive. A contract that arrives late, a few extra months of saving, or one sale falling apart near the end could each push you past the date. There is room for one small slip, not several.

And if the house becomes yours after your birthday, you pay 2% instead of nothing. On a house in your range that is roughly €6,400 to €7,200calc — about the same as all the cash you spent step one saving. So treat May 2028 as the latest comfortable date, not the goal. If you can be ready earlier, be ready earlier.

III The four steps

One after the other. Each step pays for the next.

Step one

Save for the house

Now until May 2028 · about 21 months

If you buy a house to live in, the bank can lend you the full price. All of it. What the bank will not pay are the extra costs on top: the notary, the valuation, and the mortgage advice. That part has to be your own money.4

So step one is really about one fairly small amount: €6,000 to €10,000. Over 21 months, reaching €10,000 means saving about €480 a month. If you want €15,000, so you can also pay a buying agent and keep something spare, that is about €715 a month.calc

The other half of step one is free: sort out your contract. Banks treat a permanent contract very differently from a temporary one. If yours has not changed yet, ask for an intentieverklaring — a letter from your employer saying they plan to make it permanent. Banks accept that instead.6

Two other things change how much you can borrow. Student debt counts against you, but only the amount you pay each month now, not the original loan.6 And do not buy any Dutch property before this one. The starter tax break works only once, and any use spends it. A house you own in another country does not matter — the only question is whether you have used this Dutch break before.2,3

Step two

Buy the first house, before you turn 35

Aiming for May 2028 · last chance: your 35th birthday

If you are under 35, buying a house to live in, and you have never used the break before, you pay no transfer tax at all instead of 2%. There is a price limit, but it is far above what you will spend.1,2

You claim it at the notary, on the day the house legally becomes yours. So it is that day which has to come before your birthday — not the day you sign the contract.2

Your loan will also be small enough for NHG, the national mortgage guarantee. You pay a small one-off fee. In return you get the lowest interest rate, and cover if you ever have to sell after losing your job, getting ill, or a divorce.5

Buying a house you will not keep

It helps to say out loud that this house is temporary, because it changes what "good enough" means. You are not looking for the house you keep forever. You are looking for a house worth owning during the years while the tax break still exists.

Three things follow from that.

  • Using the tax break now is the only way to use it. It ends at 35 either way. You cannot save it for a nicer house later, because later it is gone. Your second house will cost 2%, and that is simply the price of moving up.1
  • You can sell whenever you want. Renting it out is what may be blocked. Many towns have opkoopbescherming: for four years after you buy, you cannot rent the house out without a permit. It does not force you to live there, and it does not stop you selling. Your mortgage will have its own rule about living in the house too.8,9
  • Moving again means paying the extra costs again. So pick a house you are happy in for a few years, not the cheapest one that beats the deadline.

Careful

Anything you bid above the valuation, you pay in cash

The bank lends 100% of what the house is valued at — not 100% of what you agreed to pay. If you win by offering more than the valuation, you pay that difference yourself, on top of the other costs.4

This is why you save more than the bare minimum. If you save exactly the minimum, you cannot compete for any house that someone else also wants.

Step three

Save again, then the cafe

After the house · slower, and it takes longer

Here the money works the other way round. The house was cheap to start because the bank paid for almost all of it. The cafe is the opposite. Almost every euro is your own, or borrowed at a high rate, and the amounts are much bigger.

The cheapest way in is to rent a place that is already built as a cafe and take it over. Even then you need about €90,000 to start, once you count the deposit, the takeover, a light refresh, and enough money to survive the first months.

Turning an empty shell into a cafe costs far more. The fit-out is the biggest cost of all: extraction, grease trap, heavier power, toilets, sound insulation. And when you rent, most of that money stays in the building when you leave.12

Where you save the most

Find a place where the kitchen and the extraction already work. That single choice avoids the biggest cost in the whole project. An empty shell looks cheaper per square metre, but it almost always costs more in the end.

Rent the place. Do not buy it.

If you buy business premises you pay 10.4% transfer tax. That is the rate for anything that is not a home, and it stayed the same when the house rates changed. You cannot borrow it, and you never get it back. On top of that, banks lend much less against business property than against a house.1

Banks also want two or three years of trading figures before they lend against a building. You will not have those on day one.11

Renting is not a weak position here. Cafes and bars fall under a protected part of Dutch rental law: five years, then another five almost automatically, with strong rights for you as the tenant. Ten years is long enough to build something real.14

Careful

Business debt can reach your house

If you trade as a sole trader, you and the business are the same person in law, so business debts are your debts. Even with a company, banks usually ask a first-time owner for a personal guarantee — and by then your house is the most valuable thing you have to offer.10

This is not a reason to skip the cafe. It is a reason to read what you sign, and to keep two things apart: borrowing money for the cafe, and putting the house behind the cafe.

Step four

Later

Once you own the house and the cafe is running

Left open on purpose. By then you will know things you cannot know now: whether the cafe makes money, what the house is worth, and whether you still want to run a cafe at all.

The likely choices are a bigger house, buying the cafe you have been renting, a second property, or simply having less debt. None of these can be decided today, so there is nothing to tick here.

Three things will still be true whenever you get there.

  • Trading figures are what open up business lending. Two or three years of numbers changes what a bank will do for you.11
  • Government-backed loans for small businesses have a limit. Above that, you need a normal bank.11
  • Buying a house to rent out is a different world from buying one to live in: you can borrow less, you pay more tax, and there are more rules. Those rules also keep changing.1,8

IV Money

Three things about the cash that are easy to get wrong.

What the cash is for

It is not a deposit. For a first house you do not really need one, because the mortgage covers the price. You are saving for the costs you cannot borrow, and they all fall due on the day you get the keys.4

The house and the cafe use the same savings

There is only one pot of money. That is the real reason the order matters. Buying the house first uses a small amount of cash and leaves you owning something. Starting the cafe first would use everything, and you would still be renting.

Timing matters too. The tax break ends at 35. And a new business makes banks lend you less, not more, because they judge self-employed income on years of accounts instead of a payslip. So starting the business first makes the mortgage harder in exactly the years when the tax break still works.6

Why buying to rent out is not the first move

A house bought to rent out is worse in every way at the start. You can borrow less against it. Transfer tax is 8% instead of nothing. There is no starter break and no NHG. The tax on it is heavier. And rents are capped at this price level. It also does not solve your own housing, because you would still pay rent somewhere else.1,8

It is a fair option later, once you own a house and have built up value in it. It is not a way to begin.

V Words you will meet

The Dutch terms, in roughly the order you will run into them.

Transfer tax: the four rates

It is one tax with four rates, depending on what you buy and what you use it for. Mixing them up is the most expensive mistake in this plan.

One thing to know: in 2026 the rate for a house you do not live in dropped to 8%, while the 10.4% rate for anything that is not a house stayed the same. That is why older advice you find online often disagrees with itself.1

Glossary

kosten koper — buyer's costs
The costs on top of the price: notary, valuation, advice, and transfer tax if you owe any. Always your own money, never the mortgage. A listing marked k.k. means you pay these.
startersvrijstelling — starter tax break
No transfer tax instead of 2%. You must be under 35 on the day the house becomes yours, live in it yourself, never have used the break before, and stay under a price limit. You sign for it at the notary.2
NHG — national mortgage guarantee
A government guarantee you can get on loans below a certain size. You pay a one-off fee. You get the lowest interest rate, and it can cover leftover debt if you are forced to sell after job loss, illness, divorce or death.5
intentieverklaring — letter of intent
A letter from your employer saying they intend to give you a permanent contract. Banks accept it instead of the contract itself, which makes it the cheapest way to unblock a mortgage.
opkoopbescherming — buy-up protection
A town rule that stops you renting the house out without a permit for four years after you buy. Common in cities. It does not force you to live there, and it does not stop you selling.8
Starterslening — starter loan
An extra loan from the town, on top of your mortgage, with nothing to pay each month in the first years. Amounts differ per town, the money runs out, and it usually has to be your first house.7
VvE — owners' association
Buy an apartment and you join it automatically. Its money, its savings fund and its rules become yours too, so they are part of what you are buying.
Sociale Hygiëne — hospitality diploma
Needed for an alcohol licence. It lasts for life, and you can get it years before you have any building.15
casco — empty shell
A space with nothing fitted in it. Cheaper per square metre, and usually more expensive in total, because you pay for every installation yourself.

What this page cannot tell you

  • Your real maximum loan. Only an adviser can give you that, and it depends on your debts and on the bank.6
  • Which town. It decides the price, the starter loan and the renting rules all at once.9
  • Whether your residence status affects any of this. Nothing here checks it.
  • Whether you buy alone or with someone. Two salaries change the sums, and both names on the deed has consequences worth an hour with an adviser. The plan works either way.
